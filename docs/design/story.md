# Làng Vọng — The Story

Nov 1, 2026 · @catlyn

*The teller is an old woman who lived three doors down from Sen's family. She was a young wife back then. She left Làng Vọng with the last families, and she's telling this to her grandson on Rằm tháng bảy while they fold paper lanterns. She saw some of it, heard some of it, and guesses at the rest, and she says which is which. Each numbered tale works on its own as a cut scene, and her voice can be the voice-over.*

## 1. Fold it slower

No, no, fold it slower, or the corners tear. There. Like that.

You want to know why I always make one extra? Every year, one more lantern than we have dead. Your mother thinks I'm getting confused. I'm not confused. Sit down, the mosquito coil's lit, and I'll tell you about Làng Vọng. You've seen it on your phone already, haven't you? That boy's video. Well, the video doesn't know the half of it.

## 2. The lantern village

When I married your grandfather, I moved to his village on the lake. Làng Vọng. You couldn't get there easily. There was the causeway over the marsh, which flooded half the rainy season, or there was the market boat, which came across the lake once a week with rice, fish sauce, and the mail. Everyone ran down to the dock when they heard its engine.

It was a poor village, but oh, child, on Rằm tháng bảy there was nowhere like it.

You know what the fifteenth of the seventh month is. The gates open, and the dead who have no grave and nobody left to burn incense for them are let out to wander. My mother-in-law used to say a lost soul is like a child in the dark. It can't find the way home unless someone holds up a light.

So the whole village made lanterns. We carried them from the old shrine by the water all the way round to the puppet stage on the far shore, and while the puppets splashed and the drums went, we set them on the lake. Hundreds of them. They'd drift out to the middle and sit there together, like a little town made of light.

All that, for strangers. For souls we had never met. I was so proud of it.

## 3. The quiet house

Three doors down from us, at the very end of the last lane, there was a house that never came.

A man lived there, his wife Thu, and their little girl. He didn't let them out much. No festival, no weddings, no sitting on the step in the evening to talk like the rest of us did. Most days you'd forget there was anyone in there.

Some nights, you couldn't forget.

I won't tell you what it sounded like. You're too young, and I don't want to remember it that clearly. I'll tell you what we did, which is worse. Your grandfather would reach over and turn the radio up. Next door they did the same. You could hear the radios go up all along the lane, one after another, like frogs starting up after rain.

"It's their family business," we said. *Chuyện nhà người ta.* And we went to sleep.

I turned up the radio too, child. I want you to know that before I go any further.

## 4. Sen and the carp

The little girl was called Sen. Skinny thing, eight years old, hair always in her eyes.

Behind their house there were old stone steps going down into a cove. You couldn't get to them except through their back yard, and you couldn't see them from anywhere in the village. But from the bottom step you could see the whole lake, right across to the far shore. I only know because I went down there once, years later. I'll come to that.

The carp came to those steps. Sen fed them every day. She'd sneak out with rice bran in a tin. She gave every single one of them a name, and she talked to them. Hours, sometimes. I'd hear her voice coming over the fence, very small, telling the fish about her day. They were the only ones she had to tell.

I'll tell you three things that girl never had. No marks on the doorframe, because no one ever measured how tall she was getting. No name written down anywhere in that village. And no lantern of her own. On festival nights she sat on those steps and watched ours go by.

## 5. Thu's story

The only place Thu went on her own was the well. That's where I got to know her, a little. Pulling up buckets side by side, the way women do.

She'd married in from a village far across the lake, same as me, so we had that. She couldn't read. Plenty of women our age couldn't. Whatever letters came to that house, her husband read them to her, or he didn't, and she never knew the difference.

But she could tell a story. One morning at the well she told me the one she told Sen at bedtime. I've never forgotten it.

*On festival night, the lanterns carry the lost souls across the lake, to a happier place on the other side. And the carp swim beside them to show the way.*

"She believes it," Thu said, and laughed a little, the way you laugh when it isn't funny. "She tells the fish to take her with them."

I laughed too. Heaven forgive me, I laughed.

## 6. The fish lantern

The summer Sen turned eight, I used to see her on the back step folding something out of scraps of paper. A fish. A lumpy little paper carp. I thought it was a toy. I didn't understand until much later that she was making a lantern. Her first one.

That ghost month, word came on the market boat that Thu's mother was dying, back in her own village. Thu had to go. I was at the dock when she left. She grabbed my wrist, hard, harder than you'd think a woman that small could grip, and she said, "Look in on her for me. Just look in on her."

I said yes.

I didn't.

## 7. Festival night

That Rằm tháng bảy, I took your uncle to the festival for the first time. He was a baby, you understand, he doesn't remember. The whole village was on the far shore. The drums, the puppets, everybody shouting. The music came off that water so loud you could feel it in your teeth.

And on our side of the lake, in the quiet house, it was the worst night of all. Nobody heard a thing. How could we? We were all over there, making noise for the dead.

Sen ran. Out the back, across the yard, down to her steps with her paper fish. She lit it and put it on the water. To tell the carp she was ready, I think. She leaned out to push it further, and the stone was wet.

The cove was hidden. Nobody saw her go in.

Well. Almost nobody. Years later, at a funeral, the puppet master had too much rice wine and told me something he'd never told anyone. That night, from his stage, he'd seen one lantern come drifting out from behind the last house on the lane, where no lantern had any business coming from. He'd watched it join the others in the middle of the lake. He'd thought, *that's strange.*

And he said nothing.

So that's two silences: the radios, and the puppet master. The third one is the worst.

## 8. The empty steps

This part, nobody saw. But I lived three doors from that man for twelve years, so I'll tell it the way it must have been.

He came looking for her late, after the house had gone quiet. He found the steps empty. One small sandal on the bottom step. And way out on the water, one little paper fish, still lit, drifting toward the others.

He understood. Of course he understood. And then he thought about us. What the village would say. Why an eight-year-old ran out alone into the dark on festival night, and who she was running from.

He didn't shout. Child, if he had shouted, the whole village was right there on the water with boats. We'd have come. Every one of us would have come.

He didn't shout. He picked up the sandal, went inside, and shut the door.

In the morning he told us he'd sent Sen to his sister in the city, to go to a proper school. And do you know what I said? I said, "Good. Good for her." I was glad. I thought she'd got out.

Nobody floated a lantern for Sen. Why would we? As far as we knew, she was in the city, learning her letters.

## 9. The letter

Thu came home after her mother's funeral and Sen was gone. "With my sister in the city," he told her. "At school."

And Thu was relieved. I saw it on her face at the well. Her girl was out of that house. She asked him to write to Sen for her and send her love, since she couldn't write it herself.

The fish seller told me the rest, years later, when we'd both moved to town and ran into each other at the market. A few months after the festival, the boat brought a letter from his sister. She handed it to him over her baskets, and he read it right there at her stall, and she said his face went the color of ash. All he said was, "Don't mention this to Thu."

And she didn't. You see how they pile up? One more silence.

I didn't know what the letter said until I saw it in that boy's video. One line: *Sen never came here. Where is she?*

When Thu asked him what his sister had written, he said Sen was doing well. Then he folded that letter up very small and hid it under the family altar, under the ancestors' photographs, where he knew she'd never look.

## 10. The lake keeps them

You've heard me say it a hundred times: don't swim alone. Here is why.

When someone drowns, the old people say, their soul becomes a *ma da*. It stays in that water, cold and alone, and the only way it can be born again is if someone else drowns in its place. So it waits by the edge, and it reaches.

But Sen didn't want to be born again. Think about it. Born again means a new family, and what if it was the same? So when she reached for another child, it wasn't to trade places with them. It was to keep them. To have a friend, finally.

The boy's video shows something down under the middle of the lake, where all the lanterns gather: a festival. Puppets, lights, children sitting in rows. If you believe it. I believe it. It's the festival she watched from her steps every year and was never allowed to go to.

The first child was Minh, the teacher's boy, who ran to the water after a scolding over his marks. Then Hạnh, the puppet master's girl, who liked to play by herself under the stage. Then Tí, the fish seller's little one. That child blew a clay whistle all day long, drove us all mad. One afternoon the whistle stopped, and it never started again. The last was Khoa, the village head's son, fishing by himself at dusk.

Years apart, every one of them. Every one of them alone by the water, the way she'd been. We never found a single one. "The lake took them," we said. And every Rằm we floated a lantern with their names.

## 11. The vote

The year after the festival, on Rằm night, Sen's father went down to those steps by himself, and he didn't come back. People said he'd gone to the city for work. In the boy's video they found his jacket tangled in the reeds at the bottom of the lake, so I suppose the lake has its own opinion.

One by one, the families who'd lost a child fell apart. You can't live in a house where your child's sandals are still by the door. They had other children to keep away from the water. They left.

In the end the whole village met at the communal house to vote on stopping the festival and leaving for good. Everyone blamed the lake.

I stood up. I had never stood up in a meeting in my life. I said, "What about Thu's girl? Has anyone ever had a letter from Thu's girl?"

And the village head said, "Sit down. That's their business."

And I sat down.

Before he left, he wrote out the names of every lost child on a sheet of red paper for the shrine. Sen's name wasn't on it. I saw. I didn't say anything about that either.

## 12. The one who stayed

Thu went to the city to find her. But she didn't know the sister's address, and she couldn't have read it if she had. She went market to market, school to school, asking for an eight-year-old girl from Làng Vọng. Nobody had seen her.

She came back and said Sen must have run off from the aunt's, and one day she'd find her way home. That's what she decided. That's what she needed to be true.

We were one of the last families to go. I stood at Thu's gate with my bundles and said, "Come with us. Come on." She shook her head. "If I go," she said, "who'll be here when she comes home?"

I never went back.

She looked after the shrine, after that. All those photos the families left behind. Grieving other people's children is easier than grieving your own; I know that now. And every night of ghost month, she made a lantern for each lost child and set it on the water. She couldn't write, so she copied each name off the village head's red paper at the shrine, stroke by stroke, the way you'd copy a drawing. Each lantern drifted to the shore below that child's house, and the window lit up, for one night.

Not one for Sen. Her name wasn't on the red paper, so Thu never had it to copy. And to make one anyway would have meant saying her daughter was dead.

Someone in town told me, years later, that she died in that house. They found her in her chair, facing the door. Nobody knows how long she'd been sitting there.

But she didn't stop waiting. That's the thing. Every ghost month the lanterns still go out on an empty lake. That's the rumor those children heard, the one that brought them there: in Làng Vọng, the lanterns light themselves.

## 13. The boy's video

The rest I know from the video. You showed it to me yourself, on your phone, at Tet. You thought it was just a scary video. Then you saw my face.

Four young people who make ghost films. They'd heard the rumor about the lanterns, and they brought a fifth friend, a boy called Bin, to carry the camera. They walked in along the old causeway at dusk on the fourteenth day of ghost month.

At the shrine, there was an old woman sitting among the lanterns, folding. And I knew her. I knew her the second the camera found her. Older, bent over, but Thu. I had to put the phone down for a minute.

She was so glad to see them. She poured them tea. She told them about the children the lake had taken, and about the festival we used to have. And then, almost to herself, she told them the bedtime story. The lanterns, the carp, the happier place. Word for word, the way she told it to me at the well.

"My daughter's staying with relatives," she said. "She'll be home soon."

That night they filmed her lanterns going out across the lake. One by one, each lantern stopped below a house, and that house's window lit up. Three doors down from where I used to live, one house stayed dark. They didn't think anything of it. Why would they?

Then the boy Bin went off by himself to film the water, and he didn't come back. The others ran for the causeway and it was under water. Every path out of the village brought them back to the lake.

They found Bin's camera floating in the reeds by the shrine, still recording. The last thing on it was a set of stone steps going down into dark water, and at the bottom, a little girl standing in the water, looking up. Then the picture breaks up, and when it comes back she's closer. And closer. A small hand comes up and takes his. The camera goes into the water, and there's no sound at all, not even a shout. Through all that static you can just make out the two of them, the girl and the boy, going down together into the dark.

I knew those steps too.

## 14. Who is she?

They went house to house looking for Bin, and every house told them a bit more about the girl.

In the teacher's house: a class photo, and at the end of the row, a skinny girl with her hair in her eyes, not smiling. Under her, in the teacher's handwriting, *Sen*. And in his drawer, a report he'd written about her, saying something was wrong in that house. He never sent it.

In the puppet master's house: his diary, and the lantern from behind the last house. So he did tell someone, in the end. Only the paper.

In the fish seller's house: her notes about a letter from the city, and a man saying, "Don't mention this to Thu."

In the village head's house: the minutes from that meeting. My own question was in there, child. *What about Thu's girl?* And under it, one line: *Told to sit down.*

And at the shrine, the red paper. Every lost child's name, except one. Every child had a photo, except one. Every lantern the old woman made had a name, except that one.

Then they remembered what she'd told them: a lantern guides a soul home. And they remembered their footage. Every house lit up but one.

So they went to the dark house. No marks on the doorframe. A little bowl and chopsticks at one place at the table, freshly washed. In the back room, an old woman's chair turned to face the door, and teacups gray with years of dust. The same cups they'd drunk from at the shrine. A paper fish lantern, half finished, on the shelf.

Then they played back their footage from the shrine, and there was no old woman in it. Only them, talking to an empty mat, drinking from dusty cups.

That's where I stopped the video and cried. Because then I knew. Thu had been dead for years, and she was still sitting there pouring tea for strangers, still waiting for her girl.

Through the back yard, they found the stone steps. The carp came up when they climbed down, the way they used to for Sen. There were little sandals lined up on the steps, one pair for every lost child. And back up in the house, under the altar, under the ancestors' photos, was the letter: *Sen never came here. Where is she?*

That's when they understood. The girl in the water was Sen. She'd never gone anywhere. And the one person who could bring her home was sitting at the shrine, making lanterns for everyone's child but her own.

## 15. The name on the lantern

On Rằm night they took the letter to the shrine. They read it out to her, because she couldn't read it herself.

She listened for a long time. The camera was shaking; whoever held it was crying too, I think. And then Thu stopped smiling, and she cried for her daughter. Thirty years, and that was the first time.

She took out that half-finished paper fish and finished it, right there, her hands going so fast. Then she held it out to them and asked them to write the name on it. The same thing she used to ask her husband: *write to Sen for me.* So one of them wrote it. *Sen.* The first time that name was ever written anywhere in Làng Vọng.

They walked her home, through her own back yard, down to the steps. At midnight she put the lantern on the water. It went out of the cove, past the reeds, all the way to the middle where the lanterns gather. The water moved. And Sen came up, holding hands with every child she'd taken. Minh. Hạnh. Little Tí. Khoa. And the boy, Bin.

Thu walked down the steps and out onto the water like it was the floor of her own house, and she took her daughter's hand, and said her name.

That was all Sen ever wanted, I think. Someone to say her name and hold on to her. She let the other children go. The carp came up all around, hundreds of them, more than I ever saw in my life, and they carried every lantern across the lake. And the mother and the little girl followed that lumpy paper fish together, out into the dark, to the happier place on the other side. Just like the bedtime story said.

In the morning the causeway was dry, and Bin woke up on the bank with his camera in his arms. Back in the quiet house, the old radio that had been hissing for thirty years clicked off on its own. They went home. And Bin put it all online.

That was the first time anyone ever told what happened in Làng Vọng. It took four strangers and a boy with a camera. Not one of us.

## 16. How other people tell it

Now, people online argue about it. Some of them say that's not how it ended at all.

Some say they found Sen but never took the truth to her mother. So Thu floated her lanterns from the stage like every year, none for Sen, and Sen came up at the steps alone. She reached up and took the nearest hand, and as she pulled, the other children started to drift loose, the boy Bin too. Each of them had a moment to choose: hold on to her, or let go. The ones who held on went down with her, and for their sake she let the others go. Some say you can see new sandals on those steps now. Bin made it home, and so did the ones who let go, and they told the story, and missed their friends every day of their lives.

And some say they never worked it out at all. That every lantern on the lake went out at once, and the quiet came down over the whole village, and Sen came for them one by one until morning. That Bin was never found. That weeks later somebody found a camera on some old steps by an abandoned lake, with nothing on it but static and, very faint, festival music. They put it online, and it became the rumor, and the rumor brought the next ones.

I don't know which one is true. I know which one I pray for.

## Fold it slower

So. Now you know why I make one extra.

Here. You write the name on it. Your hand's steadier than mine. S, E, N. That's right.

And listen to me. If you ever hear a house go quiet, the wrong kind of quiet, don't you dare turn up the radio. You go and knock on that door.

Go on. Take it down to the water.
